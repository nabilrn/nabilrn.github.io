I use AI a lot, enough that I no longer think the interesting question is whether people in Indonesia use it.

We do.

The harder question is what happens after the answer appears on the screen.

When I use AI for code, infrastructure, research, or writing, it is most useful when I already know enough to challenge it. I can ask for another approach, check the documentation, run the command in a safe environment, inspect the diff, or reject an answer that does not make sense. The dangerous moment is when the output looks convincing and I have no way to tell whether it is actually right.

That distinction matters more to me than how many prompts a country sends.

## Indonesia does not have an adoption problem

The numbers around Indonesian AI use are already large. In the [2024 Microsoft and LinkedIn Work Trend Index](https://news.microsoft.com/id-id/2024/06/11/microsoft-and-linkedin-release-the-2024-work-trend-index-on-the-state-of-ai-at-work-in-indonesia/), 92% of surveyed knowledge workers in Indonesia said they were already using generative AI at work, compared with 75% globally.

A year later, the [Indonesia e-Conomy SEA 2025 report](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/indonesia_e_conomy_sea_2025_report.pdf) from Google, Temasek, and Bain showed the same direction from a consumer angle. In its survey, 80% of Indonesian respondents said they interacted with AI tools and features every day, and 68% said they had conversations with and asked questions of AI chatbots. Apps marketed with AI features also recorded 127% revenue growth in the first half of 2025 compared with the first half of 2024.

Even public sentiment is unusually positive. The [Ipsos AI Monitor 2025](https://www.ipsos.com/en-id/press-release-ipsos-ai-monitor-2025) reported that 91% of Indonesian respondents said they had a good understanding of AI and 80% were excited about AI-powered products and services.

That is why I no longer like the claim that Indonesians simply "do not understand AI." It is too broad, and the evidence does not support saying that about an entire country. Self-reported understanding is not the same thing as passing a technical competency test, but it would be equally lazy to ignore the amount of adoption, experimentation, and confidence that already exists here.

The more useful question is whether that confidence is matched by judgment.

## Using AI and evaluating AI are different skills

Knowing the names of ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, or whatever model is popular this month is not what I mean by AI literacy. I care more about whether someone can recognize when a model is guessing, when a source is missing, when a command is risky, or when a polished paragraph is hiding a weak argument.

This is where Indonesia's situation becomes more interesting. UNESCO and Indonesia's communications ministry completed an [AI Readiness Assessment](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/unesco-and-kominfo-completed-ai-readiness-assessment-indonesia-ready-ai?hub=378) in 2024 after consultations across five regions. The assessment did not describe a country that was simply "behind." It found specific gaps: awareness that AI can reinforce bias and discrimination was relatively low, AI research funding remained weak compared with neighboring countries, and the report called for stronger public education and institutional coordination.

That framing makes more sense to me. The problem is not that people are using AI. The problem appears when use grows faster than the habits and institutions needed to verify it.

A confident wrong answer is still wrong. If an AI invents a regulation, misreads a technical document, suggests a destructive command, or produces a statistic with no real source, someone has to catch it. The model cannot be the final authority on its own output.

## The MIT study is a warning, not a statistic about Indonesia

One study that gets quoted heavily in discussions like this is the 2025 MIT Media Lab paper [*Your Brain on ChatGPT*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872). It is interesting, but it is also easy to misuse.

The study involved 54 participants across its first three essay-writing sessions, split between an LLM group, a search-engine group, and a brain-only group. EEG measurements showed the LLM group had the weakest brain connectivity of the three conditions, and the researchers also found weaker recall and lower reported ownership of the essays among LLM users. Only 18 participants completed the fourth session, where some participants switched conditions.

That is worth paying attention to. It is **not** evidence that some percentage of Indonesian AI users cannot remember their work, and it is not a license to turn a small, specific essay-writing experiment into a claim about an entire population or every kind of AI-assisted task.

I care more about the practical lesson than the headline. If I ask AI to produce something and then cannot explain the result after closing the chat, I probably outsourced more thinking than I intended. If I can inspect it, question it, test it, and improve it, the tool is doing something much more useful.

## High consumer momentum does not mean there is nothing to build

There is also a temptation to split countries into "AI builders" and "AI consumers" as if the distinction were simple. I used to find that framing convincing, but the data is messier.

The e-Conomy SEA 2025 report estimated $91 million in private AI funding for Indonesia from the second half of 2024 through the first half of 2025, equal to 4% of AI investment across ASEAN-10 during that period. At the same time, Indonesia led the region in revenue growth for apps marketed with AI features.

Those two numbers describe different things. One is a snapshot of private investment; the other is commercial adoption. Neither tells us, by itself, how many capable AI engineers Indonesia has, how strong its research community is, or how well organizations are deploying AI internally.

UNESCO's finding that AI research remains underfunded is a better reason to care about the builder side of the ecosystem. I would like to see more people here who can train, evaluate, integrate, secure, and operate AI systems, not because using a finished product is somehow inferior, but because deeper capability gives us more control over where the technology goes.

## What better AI use looks like to me

I do not think the answer is to use AI less. I think the standard for using it should be higher.

When AI helps me with code, the output still has to survive the compiler, tests, documentation, and the actual architecture of the project. When it suggests infrastructure commands, I need to know what those commands will touch before I run them. When it gives me a factual claim, I should be able to open the original source instead of trusting a citation-shaped sentence. When it helps with writing, I still need to decide what I actually believe.

The same principle works outside engineering. AI can accelerate the boring part, expose options, explain unfamiliar concepts, and make information easier to reach. But the user still owns the decision.

Indonesia's fast AI adoption is not something I see as a weakness. It can be an advantage. Millions of people becoming comfortable with a new class of tools creates room for new products, new workflows, and new kinds of work.

But usage numbers are only the beginning. The part that compounds over time is whether people become better at reasoning with the tool, checking it, and eventually building with it.

That is the gap I care about: not between people who use AI and people who do not, but between **using AI because it can answer** and **using AI while still knowing how to judge the answer**.
