> *"The best technology is often the one that works quietly behind the scenes. We use it every day, but almost never know who created it."*

If I ask, **"Have you ever heard of Linux?"**, chances are most people will say they have.

Especially if you work or study in the tech field. The name Linux has almost certainly come up, whether while learning programming, managing servers, using the cloud, running Docker, or simply hearing the name Ubuntu.

But if I follow up by asking, **"Who created Linux?"**, I am sure the number of people who can answer will be far fewer.

His name is **Linus Torvalds**.

Funnily enough, I only truly got to know this figure in the last few years. Even though almost every day I use technology born from his work.

Initially, I thought Linus was the typical tech founder who often appears in the media. I imagined someone who built a billion-dollar company, frequently gave keynotes at various conferences, or actively built a personal brand on the internet.

Turns out I was wrong.

What I found instead was a very simple software engineer. He doesn't talk much about himself. He doesn't chase popularity. He isn't busy selling dreams.

He just enjoys solving problems.

The more I read his story, the more I realize that the tech world isn't solely built by massive companies like Google, Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, or Meta. Behind all that are thousands or even millions of engineers working without much recognition. They write code, fix bugs, build foundations, and then share them so others can build something bigger.

Many names deserve respect, such as Richard Stallman with the GNU Project, Tim Berners-Lee who created the World Wide Web, Guido van Rossum with Python, or Brendan Eich with JavaScript.

But to me personally, Linus Torvalds always holds a special place.

Not because Linux is the most popular operating system.

Not because Git became the industry standard either.

But because of his way of thinking.

He never seemed like someone trying to change the world.

He just kept solving one problem after another.

Ironically, it's exactly that kind of person who eventually truly changed the world.

## A Hobby Project That Changed the World

Let's step back to 1991.

At that time, Linus Torvalds was still a computer science student at the University of Helsinki, Finland.

Like many other engineering students, he enjoyed experimenting with computers. One of the operating systems he used back then was **MINIX**, a UNIX-based operating system created by Professor Andrew Tanenbaum for educational purposes.

MINIX was great for learning.

However, Linus felt the operating system had many limitations. There were many things he wanted to change, but couldn't do so freely.

Most people might have stopped there.

Complained.

Or waited for someone else to fix it.

But Linus chose a different path.

He started writing his own operating system.

Not to be sold.

Not to be turned into a startup.

Not because he wanted to be famous either.

He just wanted to make an operating system that worked according to his own needs.

Come to think of it, that reason sounds very simple.

But history often begins with simple reasons.

On August 25, 1991, Linus sent a message to a Usenet forum that is now one of the most famous quotes in open-source history.

> *"I'm doing a (free) operating system (just a hobby, won't be big and professional like GNU)."*

That sentence might be one of the most inaccurate predictions in the history of technology.

*"Just a hobby."*

Today, it is almost impossible to imagine a world without Linux.

After its source code was opened to the public, developers from various countries started contributing.

Some fixed bugs.

Some added drivers.

Some improved performance.

Some wrote documentation.

Some helped with testing.

One person turned into ten.

Ten became a hundred.

A hundred became thousands.

Then tens of thousands.

Linux slowly transformed from a personal project into one of the largest collaborative projects in software history.

Interestingly, Linus didn't try to do everything himself.

He understood that a project this huge could only grow if many people took ownership and contributed to it.

Perhaps this is one of the reasons why Linux has survived for over three decades and continues to grow today.

## Linux is Everywhere, Even if We Rarely Notice It

If asked, "How often do we use Linux?"

The answer is probably more often than we think.

Most of the websites we access every day run on Linux servers.

The majority of modern cloud services use Linux as their foundation.

AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, Oracle Cloud, and many other cloud providers run most of their infrastructure on Linux.

Every Android phone in the world also uses the Linux kernel.

This means billions of smartphones used every day have Linux at the core of their operating system.

It doesn't stop there.

Linux is also used in the world's fastest supercomputers.

Used in routers and switches that connect internet traffic.

Used in smart TVs, IoT devices, cameras, industrial systems, and various embedded devices that we might not even realize.

Ironically, most tech users never see the Linux logo.

They just open applications.

Watch YouTube.

Send messages.

Use social media.

Save files to the cloud.

Use AI services.

All of that feels entirely normal.

Even though behind the scenes, there's a huge chance a Linux server is working endlessly.

In my opinion, this is the definition of a truly successful technology.

Not the most frequently promoted technology.

But technology that becomes so essential that its presence feels "invisible."

When a foundation works incredibly well, people no longer think about the foundation.

They simply enjoy the building that stands on it.

And Linux is one of the greatest foundations of the modern digital world.

## Not Satisfied With Just Linux, Linus Created Git

If Linus's story stopped at Linux, I believe his name would still be etched in technology history.

But it turned out his contributions were not yet finished.

Around 2005, Linux development already involved thousands of developers from all over the world.

A new problem emerged.

How do thousands of people work on millions of lines of the same code without making a complete mess?

The existing version control tools at the time were inadequate to meet the needs of Linux development.

Some people might look for alternatives.

Some might wait for a solution from someone else.

But Linus did the same thing as before.

He built his own solution.

The result was **Git**.

Today, almost every software engineer knows Git.

GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket, Azure DevOps, and various modern collaboration platforms are built on top of the Git ecosystem.

Branching, merging, pull requests, code reviews, commit histories—all concepts that feel commonplace to modern developers today are made much easier because of Git.

It is hard to imagine what the development of open source would be like without Git.

Thousands of projects with millions of contributors around the world can develop more methodically because of this tool.

Interestingly, Git was also not born because Linus wanted to create a new product.

Git was born because there was a real problem that needed solving.

The more I read about his life journey, the more I see the same pattern.

There's a problem.

He understands the problem.

Then he builds a solution.

Not to be famous.

But because the problem is genuinely worth solving.

## The Lessons I Learned

The more I learn about Linus Torvalds, the more I realize that the biggest lesson isn't Linux or Git.

The biggest lesson is about **mindset**.

In an era where everyone races to build a personal brand, chase followers, go viral, and want to be known as "the person who made something", Linus shows a different path entirely.

He lets his work speak.

He never said that he wanted to change the world.

But the world changed because of what he built.

Honestly, learning about Linus's story makes me much more humble.

I realize that in the tech world, I am still nobody.

There are still so many things I don't know.

There are still so many great engineers whose names might never appear in the media, but their contributions support the internet we use every day.

And strangely, that realization doesn't make me feel insecure.

Quite the opposite.

It makes me even more excited to keep learning.

Because perhaps, all great contributions always start with simple curiosity.

I don't know if one day I can create something with an impact as huge as Linux or Git.

Most likely not.

But I hope that one day I can make a contribution, no matter how small, to the tech world in the same way.

Stay curious.

Stay focused on solving problems.

Stay humble.

And let the work speak louder than my own name.
